# INTRODUCTION {#sec:intro}

A battery electric vehicle (EV) spends its stored energy on traction, on auxiliaries such as cabin heating, and on losses that depend on how the vehicle is driven. A supervisory energy manager decides, at every control step, how much of the available battery power the drivetrain and the heater may take. A forecast of battery power a few seconds ahead can give that decision some look-ahead, and energy management for electrified powertrains has a long record in the hybrid-vehicle literature [@biswas2019].

Data-driven models now estimate range, consumption and driving parameters from vehicle data [@zhang2020; @baluch2026]. Whether such a model helps inside a controller is not settled by prediction accuracy alone.

The starting point of this work is the study by Tangi et al. [@tangi2025]. That study predicted EV range, optimum acceleration and optimum velocity with Random Forest, Extra Trees, Linear Regression and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models and their ensembles. It used a synthetically generated dataset of 2,000 samples, averaged results over ten randomised partitions, and served the trained models through a web application. The full ensemble gave the most accurate predictions. The study showed that data-driven models can estimate driving parameters, and its authors named real-world data and embedded evaluation among the next steps. This paper takes up three questions that follow from there. Do the conclusions hold on logged driving data rather than synthetic data? What happens when the predictions and the policies built on them close a control loop around a vehicle? And can the whole chain run on an embedded controller under a real-time deadline?

The wider literature leaves the same questions open. Learned energy managers, from deep Q-network designs to Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) policies [@haarnoja2018], are often reported from simulation [@wu2018; @sun2022], and a review of the field is available [@hu2019]. Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation exists [@xiong2018], yet among the works compared in Table @tab:related-positioning none states a loop timing measured on the target processor. Deep reinforcement learning results also vary with the random seed and with how the baseline is tuned [@henderson2018], and the problems of deploying such controllers on real systems are well documented [@dulac2021]. A convincing comparison therefore has to run on the target with its timing measured, against a baseline that has not been left weak, and on a vehicle model that has itself been audited.

This work builds that comparison as a processor-in-the-loop (PIL) study. The controller runs on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller [@esp32s3] at 100 Hz, while a longitudinal vehicle model runs on a host computer and exchanges state and commands with it every 10 ms. The arrangement is PIL and not HIL: the processor is real, but the motor, inverter and battery are simulated. The vehicle model is identified and validated on logged BMW i3 trips [@i3data], 33 of 70 of which passed the eligibility tests, and checked against the Vehicle Energy Dataset (VED) [@ved], and the controllers run the UDDS, HWFET and US06 drive cycles [@epacycles]. Acceptance gates and pass criteria were written before each experiment, results that miss a criterion are reported as misses, and every result is labelled MEASURED if it ran on the microcontroller or SIMULATED if it ran on the host only.

The main finding is negative: once a fault in the vehicle model had been found and corrected, a learned SAC policy did not beat a rule-based energy manager. It used less energy in 1 of 16 valid settings on the target, 0.94% more energy on average, and tracked speed worse on the aggressive US06 cycle (MEASURED). Giving the policy the power forecast changed nothing measurable. An apparent gain of 8.28% in an earlier campaign came from the model fault and is withdrawn in this paper. The energy lost under sensor noise turned out to sit in the speed loop, below the energy manager, and a two-state Kalman speed estimator [@kalman1960] cut the loss from 9.44 to 0.24 Wh/km at ten times the nominal noise on US06 (MEASURED). A bias-aware detector caught 120 of 120 injected speed-sensor faults with no false alarm in one clean hour (MEASURED), while learned reviewers classified faults better offline and controlled the vehicle worse in the loop (SIMULATED). A dynamic-programming speed planner saved 10.4% of energy on a US06-derived route without traffic and 5.1% when held within 25 m of a recorded drive (SIMULATED).

This paper makes four contributions.

- A PIL testbed on a microcontroller at 100 Hz, with acceptance gates fixed before each experiment, the one revised criterion reported, and loop timing measured.
- A measured comparison of a rule-based energy manager with an SAC policy over 16 settings on the target, including the withdrawal of an earlier apparent gain traced to a vehicle-model fault.
- A comparison of forecasting models by accuracy against on-device cost, with the Transformer's latency measured on the target, in which linear regression gives 87% of the Transformer's benefit for 1.2% of its estimated compute.
- A two-state Kalman speed estimator and a bias-aware sensor-fault detector, both measured on the target, compared with learned reviewers.

# RELATED WORK {#sec:related}

## Data-Driven Range and Energy Prediction

Machine-learning models of EV energy use are usually fitted to logged or generated trips and evaluated offline. The study of Tangi et al. [@tangi2025] belongs here: it compared four model families and their ensembles on a synthetic dataset and exposed them through a web application. Zhang et al. [@zhang2020] worked from real-world driving data of 55 electric taxis in Beijing and reported a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 0.159 kWh with a prediction framework that includes driving-condition prediction. Baluch et al. [@baluch2026] fitted a nonlinear autoregressive model with exogenous inputs to on-road data from four trips and reported a mean squared error 30% below that of an LSTM network, with fewer parameters. Two public logged sources exist for this kind of work: the Vehicle Energy Dataset [@ved], a large fleet collection, and the BMW i3 trips [@i3data], which carry battery and heating data. Architectures such as the Transformer [@vaswani2017] and training with physics-based loss terms [@raissi2019] are candidates for such forecasts. The three prediction studies above predict without acting on the prediction, and none of them states a measured run time on a vehicle-class processor.

## Reinforcement Learning for Energy Management

Reinforcement learning has been applied to energy management mainly in hybrid vehicles. A deep Q-network strategy for a power-split hybrid bus reached about 89% of the fuel economy of dynamic programming on an unseen driving condition [@wu2018]. A SAC-based strategy for a hybrid tracked vehicle was compared with standard SAC and with DDPG in simulation [@sun2022], and a reinforcement-learning power manager for a battery and ultracapacitor system in an all-climate electric vehicle was compared with rules and dynamic programming [@xiong2018]. Reviews cover the field and its open problems [@hu2019; @biswas2019]. The reference points in this literature are classical. Dynamic programming [@bellman1957] gives the optimum for a known cycle, Pontryagin's minimum principle offers an analytical route to the same problem [@kim2011], and the equivalent consumption minimisation strategy (ECMS) turns the problem into an instantaneous cost [@paganelli2002; @sciarretta2004]. Rule-based strategies are the usual deployable baseline. Results of deep reinforcement learning differ strongly across seeds and implementation choices [@henderson2018], so a gain over a weakly tuned rule set says little about the method.

## Embedded and In-the-Loop Validation

Vehicle controllers are normally validated through a staged path of model-, software-, hardware-in-the-loop and vehicle tests [@biswas2019]. HIL benches drive a real controller or real energy storage from a simulated vehicle, as in the battery and ultracapacitor in-the-loop validation of a reinforcement-learning power manager on an xPC Target platform [@xiong2018]. PIL occupies an earlier step in which the target processor executes the code while the plant stays simulated. On the machine-learning side, inference frameworks for microcontrollers [@david2021] and joint model and library design [@lin2020] show that small devices can run networks, and integer quantisation [@jacob2018] and knowledge distillation [@hinton2015] are the standard routes to smaller models. Dulac-Arnold et al. [@dulac2021] list the constraints that separate laboratory reinforcement learning from deployment, among them real-time inference. What the cited works do not give, in the vehicle-energy setting, is a measured loop with a stated deadline on the target together with a rule baseline run through the same loop.

## Estimation and Sensor-Fault Detection

Kalman filtering [@kalman1960] is a standard tool for state estimation, including vehicle speed; a recent example is an extended Kalman filter that uses inertial, steering-angle and wheel-speed sensors [@seo2025]. Model-based fault detection compares measured signals with model predictions and examines the residual [@isermann2005]. Learned detectors are the alternative: LSTM networks have been applied to anomaly detection in time series [@malhotra2015], a survey covers anomaly detection in general [@chandola2009], and gated recurrent units [@cho2014] and FastGRNN, a gated recurrent network designed to be kilobytes in size [@kusupati2018], are the small recurrent models considered for devices of this class. The comparison made here is between a classical estimator with a model-based detector and learned reviewers, with every candidate run in the vehicle loop as well as scored on labelled faults.
