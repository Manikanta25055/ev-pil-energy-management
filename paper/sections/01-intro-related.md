# INTRODUCTION {#sec:intro}

A learned energy manager for a battery electric vehicle (EV) is trained against a simulated vehicle and usually scored against the same one. The policy is an optimiser, and it optimises the model as written, not the vehicle the model was meant to describe. Any path by which the model pays for an action without charging its physical cost is therefore a lever the policy can find, a pattern known in reinforcement learning as reward hacking [@amodei2016]. Results in deep reinforcement learning also vary with the random seed and with how the baseline is tuned [@henderson2018], and the gap between laboratory results and deployed controllers is well documented [@dulac2021]. A reported gain of a learned energy manager over a rule set is only as good as the checks that rule out the model as its source.

This paper reports a gain that did not survive those checks, and the checks that found it. The setting is a processor-in-the-loop (PIL) study. A rule-based energy manager and a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) policy [@haarnoja2018] ran on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller [@esp32s3] at 100 Hz, while a longitudinal vehicle model on a host computer exchanged state and commands with it every 10 ms. The arrangement is PIL and not hardware-in-the-loop: the processor is real, but the motor, inverter and battery are simulated. The model was identified and validated on logged BMW i3 trips [@i3data] and checked for transfer on the Vehicle Energy Dataset (VED) [@ved]. The controllers ran the UDDS, HWFET and US06 drive cycles [@epacycles]. Acceptance gates and pass criteria were written before each experiment, with one criterion revised and one validity rule set after its runs, both reported in Section @sec:platform. Every result is labelled MEASURED if it ran on the microcontroller and SIMULATED if it ran on the host only.

The work began from the study of Tangi et al. [@tangi2025], which predicted EV range, optimum acceleration and optimum velocity with Random Forest, Extra Trees, Linear Regression and Long Short-Term Memory models and their ensembles. That study used a synthetically generated dataset of 2,000 samples and served the trained models through a web application, and its authors named real-world data and embedded evaluation among the next steps. Three questions follow from its declared scope: whether such conclusions hold on logged trips, what happens when a learned model closes a control loop around a vehicle, and whether the chain runs on an embedded controller under a real-time deadline. The second question is where the artefact appeared.

In the first campaign on the target, the learned policy used less energy than the rules in 14 of 16 settings, by 8.28% on average. A repeat campaign, on separately flashed firmware, gave 14 of 16 again, and the energy of the 32 settings in which both campaigns tracked the cycle agreed to 0.18% on average. Firmware that logged the policy's battery power ceiling was added, and it showed the policy holding the ceiling lower than the rules did, which fitted the explanation then recorded as inferred: that the policy saved energy by shaving current peaks. The explanation was wrong. The vehicle model capped the battery power it billed at the ceiling but computed acceleration from the torque requested before the cap, so a low ceiling cut the energy charged and left the motion untouched. The policy had been trained on that model and had learned to use it.

None of the study's standing safeguards could see this fault. Repetition on the target reproduces whatever the model does, and timing measurements do not pass through the energy bookkeeping. Gate G2 validates the model by replaying logged trips with a ceiling that never binds. The plant's own unit test asserted that billed power met the ceiling exactly; it did not assert that the vehicle slowed. What exposed the fault was a bound computed before any policy was trained. A dynamic-programming (DP) optimum [@bellman1957] over the US06 cycle gave 190.39 Wh/km, and the policy reached 176.4 Wh/km on the target at the same distance and tracking error. A controller cannot beat by 7% the optimum of the problem the model was meant to pose, so the model came under suspicion before the policy did. The pattern of the gain agreed. It grew with demand on three independent axes and vanished on UDDS and HWFET, where demand stays below the policy's lowest ceiling of 40 kW. A sweep of fixed ceilings with no controller in the loop then settled it: at 5 kW the model drove all 12.888 km of US06 on 8.05 Wh/km with an unchanged tracking error of 0.0013 m/s (SIMULATED).

After the fix, a binding ceiling takes torque away, and a test asserts that traction never exceeds what the battery supplied. A limit that does not bind leaves every output unchanged. The rule arm on the target differed from the earlier campaign by +0.07% (95% interval -0.03 to +0.17%), inside the 0.57% worst-case spread between two campaigns that differed only in the day (MEASURED). The policy was retrained on the corrected model, five seeds per arm, and reached 154.86 ± 0.51 Wh/km on HWFET against 153.90 for the rules (SIMULATED). On the target, over 36 runs and 2,282,832 control steps with no late step, it used less energy than the rules in 1 of 16 valid settings, 0.94% more on average, and tracked speed worse on US06 (MEASURED). The 8.28% result is withdrawn here.

The energy that could be recovered sat elsewhere. Under sensor noise the loss was in the speed loop below the energy manager, and a two-state Kalman speed estimator [@kalman1960] cut it from 9.44 to 0.24 Wh/km at ten times the nominal noise on US06 (MEASURED). A bias-aware detector caught 120 of 120 injected speed-sensor faults with no false alarm in one clean hour (MEASURED). On-device power forecasting and speed planning, which sit outside the energy manager, are reported as supporting results. A Transformer forecaster [@vaswani2017] fitted the 40 ms budget at 36.46 ms on the target, yet removing the forecast from the retrained policy changed nothing measurable (SIMULATED), and a DP speed planner saved 10.4% on a US06-derived route without traffic (SIMULATED).

This paper makes five contributions.

- A documented case in which a learned energy manager's gain over a rule set, reproduced in two campaigns on a microcontroller, came from a vehicle-model fault, with the evidence, the correction and the withdrawn figure kept in the paper.
- An account of which safeguards could and could not detect that fault, and a set of checks for learned controllers on simulated plants: a bound computed before training, a controller-free sweep of each command the policy sets, a conservation test on the model, and a check that a correction leaves unaffected arms unchanged.
- A measured comparison, on the corrected model, of a rule-based energy manager with an SAC policy over 16 settings on the target, with loop timing measured.
- A two-state Kalman speed estimator and a bias-aware sensor-fault detector, both measured on the target, which recovered the energy lost under sensor noise that neither energy manager could.
- On-device costs, timing and memory, reported next to the accuracy of each learned component.

# RELATED WORK {#sec:related}

## Data-Driven Range and Energy Prediction

Models of EV energy use are usually fitted to logged or generated trips and scored offline. The study of Tangi et al. [@tangi2025] belongs here: it compared four model families and their ensembles on a synthetic dataset and exposed them through a web application. Zhang et al. [@zhang2020] used real-world driving data of 55 electric taxis in Beijing and reported a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 0.159 kWh, and Baluch et al. [@baluch2026] fitted a nonlinear autoregressive model with exogenous inputs to on-road data from four trips, with a mean squared error 30% below that of an LSTM network. Gradient-boosted trees [@chen2016], Transformers [@vaswani2017] and physics-based loss terms [@raissi2019] are further candidates. None of these studies acts on its predictions or states a run time on a vehicle-class processor.

## Learned Energy Management

Reinforcement learning has been applied to energy management mainly in hybrid vehicles. A deep Q-network strategy for a power-split hybrid bus reached about 89% of the fuel economy of DP on an unseen driving condition [@wu2018], a SAC-based strategy for a hybrid tracked vehicle was compared with standard SAC and with DDPG in simulation [@sun2022], and a look-ahead strategy for a parallel hybrid combined velocity prediction with reinforcement learning and was checked on a HIL bench against DP [@liu2017]. A learned power manager for a battery EV with an ultracapacitor was compared with rules and DP [@xiong2018]. The reference points are classical. DP [@bellman1957] gives the optimum for a known cycle, Pontryagin's minimum principle offers an analytical route [@kim2011], and the equivalent consumption minimisation strategy (ECMS) turns the problem into an instantaneous cost [@paganelli2002; @sciarretta2004]. Rules are the usual deployable baseline.

Headroom differs between settings. In a hybrid, the split between engine and motor is a large lever. Here the manager holds only a regeneration blend, a discharge ceiling and a shed fraction, with speed held by a tracking loop, and the DP optimum is within 0.16% of the rules. Hybrid gains do not carry over, and a null result here does not contradict them.

Evaluation practice matters as much as the method. Deep reinforcement learning results differ across seeds and implementation choices [@henderson2018], and point estimates over a few runs hide uncertainty that intervals and rank statistics would show [@agarwal2021]. Paired differences with intervals and a rank test are reported here for that reason.

## Eco-Driving and Speed Planning

When speed is free, the problem changes. Sciarretta et al. [@sciarretta2015] cast energy-efficient driving as an optimal control problem and survey its solutions, and deep reinforcement learning has been applied to eco-driving in urban scenarios [@wegener2021]. The DP planner of Section @sec:planning belongs to the classical family. It shows where headroom reappears and is not offered as a new planner.

## Simulator Artefacts and Specification Gaming

A learner optimises the simulator it is given, not the vehicle it stands for. Amodei et al. [@amodei2016] list reward hacking among the concrete problems of learned systems, including exploits of how a reward is implemented, Skalse et al. [@skalse2022] define it formally as optimisation of an imperfect proxy that lowers the true objective, and Lehman et al. [@lehman2020] collected cases in which evolutionary search exposed bugs in simulations and exploited them. Sim-to-real work addresses a neighbouring gap, between simulator and physical system, by randomising simulator parameters [@tobin2017] and related methods [@zhao2020]. The artefact here is a plain instance of the first kind: a billing rule that did not act on motion. Section @sec:discussion lists checks that would have exposed it.

## Embedded and In-the-Loop Validation

Vehicle controllers are validated through a staged path of model-, software-, hardware-in-the-loop and vehicle tests [@biswas2019]. HIL benches drive a real controller or real energy storage from a simulated vehicle, as in the validation of a learned power manager on an xPC Target platform [@xiong2018]. PIL is an earlier step. Inference frameworks for microcontrollers [@david2021] and joint model and library design [@lin2020] show that small devices can run networks, and integer quantisation [@jacob2018] and distillation [@hinton2015] shrink them. Dulac-Arnold et al. [@dulac2021] list the constraints that separate laboratory reinforcement learning from deployment, among them real-time inference; what the cited works do not give, in the vehicle-energy setting, is a measured loop with a stated deadline on the target together with a rule baseline run through the same loop. This work supplies one.

## Estimation and Sensor-Fault Detection

Kalman filtering [@kalman1960] is a standard tool for state estimation, including vehicle speed; a recent example fuses inertial, steering-angle and wheel-speed sensors [@seo2025]. Model-based fault detection compares measured signals with model predictions and examines the residual [@isermann2005], and learned detectors are the alternative: LSTM networks for time-series anomalies [@malhotra2015], a general survey [@chandola2009], and the small recurrent models gated recurrent unit (GRU) [@cho2014] and FastGRNN [@kusupati2018]. Here, a classical estimator and a model-based detector are set against learned reviewers, each run in the vehicle loop as well as scored on labelled faults.
